Science of AI

I am interested in the science of AI: Why does it work as well as it does, and what are its limitations? How might those limitations be addressed?

Some general approaches to such questions are constructing mathematical theories (like physics) and trying to understand how models work and develop during training (like biology). We can do the latter from the outside, by studying a model's behaviour (as in our work on in-context learning through the Bayesian prism), or by looking into its innards, which is called mechanistic interpretability (as in InversionView).

Mathematical theory

The behaviour of language models can often be expressed by Bayesian inference. But they can also deviate from it, sometimes in very interesting ways, to do the “right” thing. How can we explain this? Solomonoff induction offers clues (see our work on emergent in-context learning and on hierarchical generalisation in transformers), but since it is computationally ineffective, something else is needed. A theory could be phenomenological, describing what models do without deriving it from how they work, much as thermodynamics describes heat without reference to molecules, as in the above works; or it could be built from first principles, as in our work on learning and generalisation in normalising flows and in recurrent neural networks. In first-principles theories, however, we can work only in restricted settings, such as one-hidden-layer networks of very large width.

AI for mathematics

Some early work of ours studied how transformers simplify polynomials, showing that step-wise solutions boost accuracy. This is akin to the famous idea of chain of thought, but applied to training data instead of in context. We also wrote an early paper on autoformalisation, building a mathematics formalisation assistant with large language models.

Open-ended learning. Mathematicians do not just solve problems; they build theories, deciding which definitions, questions and directions are worth pursuing. This judgement is often guided by curiosity and a sense of what is interesting. I am currently thinking about open-ended learning in this sense: how a learner, human or machine, decides what to do next, and whether notions such as interestingness and curiosity can be made precise enough to study and to build into AI systems.

Dataset construction

I have worked on constructing datasets for training and evaluating AI models. A recent example is a dataset for continual learning inspired by how children learn. It fills several gaps in previous datasets; for example, new skills that the model learns build upon previous ones. Another is a pipeline for generating communication among the employees of a company over a period of time. This includes emails, but could be much richer, including chat messages, meeting transcripts, pull requests, and so on. In the real world a communication dataset like this has a rich internal structure; preserving as much of this structure as possible in the synthetic dataset, and approaching realism, are the challenges we tackled in this work.

Work on dataset construction has given me an appreciation of how difficult it is. I have come to believe that most benchmarks have subtle but consequential flaws. Just as George Box said of models, most datasets are wrong, but some are useful. One obvious failure mode is errors by crowd workers (and now, increasingly, by LLMs). More interestingly, there can be subtle errors in the design itself, for example in failing to anticipate all the ways in which a problem can be solved. Capturing the complexity of the real world is not easy.

Theoretical computer science

Today the toolkit of a theoretical computer scientist covers a broad swathe: combinatorics, probability, information theory, high-dimensional geometry, algebra and more. One of the joys of working in the area is this wide range of connections (see, e.g., Avi Wigderson's book Mathematics and Computation).

Among other things, I have worked on information-theoretic problems (lower bounds for noisy broadcast, separating two models of parallel computation), combinatorial optimisation (the VPN conjecture in network design), algorithms (expanders from random spanning trees), combinatorics (the Lovász local lemma, a lower bound for sampling, a parallel search game), and machine learning and statistics (Thompson sampling, Fourier PCA and robust tensor decomposition, heavy-tailed independent component analysis).

Yet beneath this variety one sometimes finds a combinatorial core. Israel Gelfand made the point about mathematics:

The older I get, the more I believe that at the bottom of most deep mathematical problems there is a combinatorial problem.

— Israel Gelfand, lecture at the Courant Institute (1990); source

I have often found this in my own work. Simplified further, such problems can make rather interesting puzzles. Here are two examples.

Cutting a box

Take a box A1 × A2 × ⋯ × An, where each Ai is a finite set with at least two elements. Cut it into sub-boxes B1 × B2 × ⋯ × Bn in which every side Bi is a non-empty proper subset of Ai (any subset, not necessarily an interval). Show that you need at least 2n pieces.

For n = 2: if you cut a grid into combinatorial rectangles, none of which uses all the rows or all the columns, you need at least four of them.

This is a theorem of Alon, Bohman, Holzman and Kleitman (2002); the proof is very short. In our work on the query complexity of sampling, we study a q-analogue of it.

Searching boxes in parallel

There are a boxes and a slips of paper, numbered 0 to a − 1. The slips are put into the boxes in random order, one per box. A team of a players, also numbered 0 to a − 1, enter the room one at a time. Each player may open at most half of the boxes, looking for the slip with their own number, and then leaves without disturbing anything or communicating with the others. The team wins only if every player finds their own slip.

If every player opens boxes at random, the team wins with probability 2−a. Can the team do much better, say with a probability that does not go to zero as a grows?

This is a special case of a game of Gál and Miltersen, which we studied in a paper on parallel search.

Answer

Yes. Player i opens box i, then the box whose number is on the slip found there, and so on, following the cycle of the permutation that contains i. Everyone succeeds exactly when the random permutation has no cycle longer than a/2, which happens with probability about 1 − ln 2 ≈ 0.31. This strategy is due to S. Skyum. In our paper we show that this kind of correlated success persists in the general game, answering a question of Gál and Miltersen in the negative. This special case is now widely known as the 100 prisoners problem.

Some of my AI work uses ideas from theoretical computer science directly: for example, which formal languages transformers can recognise, or Solomonoff induction as a lens on in-context learning.

Looking ahead

I want to understand how complex systems work, well enough to explain and anticipate their behaviour. Many have so far resisted this: natural ones such as the cell, the brain, ecosystems and the economy, but also ones we built ourselves, such as organisations, large codebases, distributed systems and AI models. As AI agents take on more of our work, they will create many new systems of this kind, and AI may also give us new ways to understand them. What mathematical theories can we have for such systems, and what new tools will we need?

Beyond the science, I am also looking into some new questions. In the face of rapid advances in AI, questions about its effect on humanity, at both the individual and the societal level, have taken on a new urgency. Social sciences, humanities, sciences and more—all spheres of human learning—will have to come together in the dialogue on these questions. In particular, what is the future of mathematics in this new world?

The title of Paul Gauguin's masterpiece sums it up well: Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?
